Aparajita can be quick to flower. One forage factsheet says flowers can come 4–6 weeks after sowing, and in one trial the first flowers came 7–11 weeks after sowing. That's about two to three months at most.

Mine took about 8 months: somewhere between 7 and 9, depending on exactly when they were sown.

Aparajita is *Clitoria ternatea*, the butterfly pea: a perennial climber with blue flowers. I sowed two plants early this year, and for most of the year they gave me nothing but leaves. Here is what I think held them back, what I've ruled out, and what I'm doing next.

## My two plants

| Plant | Container | Climbs | First flowers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pillar plant | Thermocol fish-market box | A pillar | Late October |
| Window plant | 15-inch grow bag | A window shutter | About a week later, early November |

They stand about 4 feet apart in the same spot and get the same care:

- **Seed:** saved by a relative from the pods of their own plant, not from a packet.
- **Sown:** roughly January to March 2026.
- **Soil:** old garden soil (heavy clay) mixed with local vermicompost.
- **Food:** vermicompost only. Never urea or any other fertiliser.
- **Sun:** about 3–5 hours of direct sun.
- **Water:** once a day in summer. Now at most twice a week.
- Never pruned. No pests.

## The suspects, ranked

I can't prove any of these. This is my honest ranking.

**1. Not enough sun (main suspect).** Aparajita is normally grown in full sun, though it is moderately shade-tolerant. Mine get 3–5 hours, which is partial shade. In a shade trial of many tropical forage legumes, shade prolonged leafy growth, delayed flowering and greatly reduced seed. To be fair, the same study found *Clitoria ternatea* among the best performers under trees. So shade is the likeliest reason, but not a proven one.

**2. A cold start.** Aparajita is a tropical plant. It needs soil temperatures of 16–36 °C to establish well. I sowed in Punjab's January to March, when it is still cool: a slow start for a tropical plant.

**3. Heavy clay: probably not the cause.** I expected clay to be a problem, but the factsheet says aparajita is extremely well adapted to heavy clay. What it doesn't like is prolonged waterlogging, and a clay-filled container watered every day can stay too wet.

**Ruled out: too much nitrogen.** Mine have only ever had vermicompost: no urea, no other fertiliser.

**Why was the pillar plant a week ahead?** I can't tell. Same seed source, same care, same spot. Home-saved seed can vary a little from plant to plant, and that may be all it is.

## Myth vs reality

**Myth:** Aparajita flowers all year.

**Reality:** It can flower through the year only with enough soil moisture and no frost. In places with very cold winters and hot summers it is often grown as an annual. Punjab is exactly that kind of place, so winter cold can cut it back.

## What I'm doing now

- **Letting the pods ripen and saving the seed**, the same way my seeds came from a relative. The pods split open when dry and fling the seed, so I'll pick them as they turn dry, before they split.
- **Watering less:** only when the top few centimetres of soil feel dry (at most twice a week now).
- **No feeding.**

Saving seed matters more than usual this year, and the next section explains why.

## This winter (December–January)

If frost burns the top, I won't pull the plants out. Aparajita can regrow from the stems after a light frost, or from the base after a heavy one. I'll wait until March to see.

To be honest, regrowth after frost is more likely on established, mature, woody plants. Mine are less than a year old, so it's less certain. If they don't come back, I'll still have the seed.

## The spring test

In spring I'll sow my own saved seed in my sunniest spot. If those plants flower much sooner than these two did, sun was the main problem. If they're just as slow, I'll look again at the cold start and the soil.
