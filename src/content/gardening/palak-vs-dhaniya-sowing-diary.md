On 18 October we sowed two of the easiest winter crops there are: palak (spinach) and dhaniya (coriander). Same terrace, same day, same soil, same watering.

Twenty-four days later, the palak box looks like a small farm. The dhaniya bags look nearly empty.

This is part of my garden restart diary. I gave up gardening once because of poor results, so this time I'm writing down exactly what I do and what happens, including the failures. This post is one of those.

## What we did on 18 October

Nothing fancy. Exactly this:

- **Soil:** plain garden soil at sowing time. No compost, no vermicompost, no fertiliser. It's heavy and clay-like, with hard clods and small stones in it. (We added vermicompost later; more on that below.)
- **Palak:** sown in a large old thermocol box.
- **Dhaniya:** sown in two grow bags, 15" wide and 6" deep.
- **Seeds:** last year's seeds, for both crops, straight from the packet. Not soaked. The dhaniya seeds were crushed to split them before sowing, as everyone advises.
- **Watering:** once every 2–3 days, whenever the top of the soil started drying.

One thing went wrong on sowing day itself: one of the dhaniya bags was watered so heavily that water stood on top of the soil. The other bag drained fine.

## What happened after 24 days

The first sprouts appeared after about 5–6 days.

Around 1 November, about two weeks after sowing, we added vermicompost on top of both the palak box and the dhaniya bags. That came after the seeds had already come up, so it can help the plants grow but it can't explain how many seeds sprouted.

**Palak: a clear success.** The box is full of healthy, bright green plants with proper leaves. The seeds landed unevenly, so one side is very crowded and the middle has gaps, but the plants themselves look great. All this in lumpy clay soil, with only a vermicompost top-up after they had sprouted. (That's the palak box in the photo at the top.)

**Dhaniya: a poor result.** Only about 5–8 plants came up in each bag, where you'd expect a thick green carpet. The plants that did come up look healthy. There just aren't many of them. (The pink flowers in the photo are petals that fell from the sadabahar pot next to it.)

## Why did the dhaniya fail? My honest best guess

I can't prove the cause yet. But first, one thing I can rule out.

**It wasn't because the seeds were whole.** A "dhaniya seed" from the packet is actually a small fruit with two seeds inside it. Tamil Nadu Agricultural University's coriander guide says whole seeds don't germinate well and should be split into halves before sowing. We did that: the seeds were crushed before sowing. So the most common advice was followed, and it still went wrong.

That leaves these suspects:

**1. The seeds were a year old.** Older seed usually germinates less well. This is my main suspect, and the easiest to test (see below).

**2. I sowed a little late.** A kitchen-garden guide from Punjab Agricultural University's experts gives mid-September to mid-October as coriander sowing time. We sowed on 18 October, just after that window, while the days were still warm.

**3. The soil formed a hard crust.** Heavy garden soil that gets soaked and then dries turns into a hard grey layer on top. You can see it clearly in the bag that was waterlogged on sowing day. Tiny dhaniya seedlings struggle to push through a crust like that.

But here's the interesting part: the other bag, which never had standing water, didn't do much better. So waterlogging alone doesn't explain it. That points me back to the seed itself.

**4. Maybe I crushed too hard.** The advice is to split the seeds gently. If some halves were crushed too hard, they may have been damaged. I can't check this now, so I'm treating it as a small possibility.

## Myth vs reality

**Myth:** Crush the dhaniya seeds and they'll come up thick. That's the one trick you need.

**Reality:** Splitting the seeds helps, but it isn't the whole story. I split mine and still got a handful of plants. Seed age, sowing time and heavy, crusting soil matter just as much.

## What I'm doing next: a fair test

My rule for this garden is to change one thing at a time, so the result actually proves something.

- **Second sowing (8 November):** We've already sown palak and dhaniya in more grow bags, using **new seed packets** from the local market, the **same garden soil**, and the dhaniya seeds crushed just like the first time. So the main change is the seed. If the new dhaniya comes up much better, that points strongly at the old seed. It isn't a perfect test, because November is colder than October, which is why I'm also doing the test below.
- **Seed test (indoors):** I'm putting 20 of last year's dhaniya seeds, split gently, on a damp tissue in a closed box, and counting how many sprout over two weeks. No soil, no crust, no weather. If only a few sprout, the old seed was the main problem.
- **Palak:** I'm thinning the crowded side so the remaining plants have room to grow, and eating the thinnings.

Mid-November is past the recommended sowing time for dhaniya, so I'm not expecting a big harvest from the 8 November bags. But together with the tissue test, they should tell me whether the seed or the conditions were to blame.

I'll post an update in about two weeks with photos from the same angle.

## What I've learned so far

- Palak is a genuinely forgiving first crop. It came up thick in plain clay soil, with nothing added until after it sprouted.
- Splitting dhaniya seeds is good advice, but it doesn't fix old seed or late sowing.
- Don't flood the soil on sowing day. Heavy soil crusts over when it dries.
- Write everything down. Without notes, I'd have no idea which change made a difference.
