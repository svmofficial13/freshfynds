> **Quick answer:** My madhumalti (Rangoon creeper) has spent more than ten years in the same pot and the same garden soil, with nothing to climb on and almost no food apart from one dose of urea and a little vermicompost. I think those three things together keep it making leaves and thin shoots instead of flowers. The brown leaves falling right now are mostly the cold, not the plant dying.

## First: is it dying?

Probably not. Every November my madhumalti starts dropping leaves, and this year is no different. Some hang on the plant brown and curled, and the rest collect in the pot.

Madhumalti is a tropical climber. It's usually described as evergreen, but it doesn't like cold: in cold weather it can lose many of its leaves, and the roots carry on and grow back. Its flowering also slows down in winter and picks up again as the weather warms. So brown leaves in a Punjab November are not, by themselves, a reason to panic.

What does worry me is the colour of the leaves that are still green. More on that below.

## My two plants

**The old one.** I bought it from a local nursery more than ten years ago. It lives in a big clay pot (about 14–15 inches) in the same garden soil it was planted in, and gets 6–8 hours of sun. Its support broke years ago, so it now grows as a bush. In all those years it has flowered twice, both times only small, light bunches: once when it was new, and once right after it was repotted.

**The young one.** About 8–10 months ago, a new shoot came up from the roots in the old pot. I dug it out and potted it on its own in a 12-inch plastic pot. It hasn't flowered yet either. It's really a piece of the old plant, which will be useful later.

## The clues

1. **Both times it flowered, it had fresh soil.** Once when it was new from the nursery, and once right after it was repotted. In between, as the soil got old, nothing. This is my strongest clue.
2. **Pale leaves.** Even the healthy leaves are a yellowish green, not a deep green. That usually means a hungry plant.
3. **A thicket of thin stems.** Instead of a few strong main stems climbing up, the base is a bundle of thin stems, and long whippy shoots arch out and flop sideways because there's nothing to climb.
4. **Almost no food.** One dose of urea about a year ago, a little vermicompost in August, and nothing else.

![Base of the old madhumalti: many thin stems coming out of the pot, pale leaves and fallen leaves on the soil](/images/gardening/madhumalti-old-plant-base.jpg)

## Likely causes

**Old, crowded soil.** Ten years in one pot means the roots have filled it, the garden soil has packed down hard, and there's little food left in it. The one time it got fresh soil, it flowered.

**Nothing to climb.** Madhumalti is a climber, and its flowers form in clusters at the ends of its branches. Without a support, my plant's energy goes into a tangle of thin, floppy shoots instead of strong climbing growth.

**The wrong kind of food.** Urea is almost pure nitrogen, and nitrogen grows leaves. Care advice for this climber specifically says to avoid high-nitrogen fertilisers, because they push leaves instead of flowers.

What is probably *not* the problem: sunlight. Six to eight hours is plenty for this plant.

## Myth vs reality

**Myth:** Give it more urea and it will finally start flowering.

**Reality:** Urea mostly grows leaves. A madhumalti that won't flower usually needs fresh soil, room for its roots and something to climb, not more nitrogen.

## What I'm doing this winter

- **Nothing drastic.** No repotting and no hard pruning while it's cold.
- **Less water.** Only when the top few centimetres of soil are dry.
- **No urea.**
- **Clearing fallen leaves** from the soil, so they don't sit there and rot.

## My plan for spring

February–March is one of the recommended planting times for this climber in our region, so that's when I'll do the big work on the old plant:

1. Take it out of the pot, trim back the crowded roots, and replant it in fresh soil mix.
2. Thin the thicket: keep a few strong stems and remove the thin ones.
3. Give it a proper support to climb.
4. Switch to balanced feeding instead of urea alone. I'll check the doses against PAU/ICAR advice before I start and share exactly what I use.
